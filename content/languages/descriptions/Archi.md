Archi, a Lezgic outlier in close contact with two other Nakh-Daghestanian languages, Avar (Avar-Andic) and Lak (Lak), is spoken in several villages in Charoda district. Technically, Archib is an agglomeration of one main village and several former homesteads, The official census of 2010 shows a population of 1,608 people, but since then there has been a sharp decline. Reportedly, not more than 100 families remain in the village on a permanent basis (as of 2026). Several thousands of ethnic Archis live outside the village, but there is no way to assess their language proficiency. The Archis’ neighbours are Laks and Avas, and Archis have traditionally shown a high degree of multilingualism in both languages.

## Grammar notes

### Basic clause structure and the transitive construction

In terms of both case marking and gender agreement in the clause, Archi shows ergative patterning; the canonical transitive construction is observed in, e.g. (15). The ergative construction is often obscured by the binominative construction involving the converb of the lexical verb and the auxiliary, as in (15, 34, 43, 91), where the converb agrees with the P-argument (like in the canonical transitive construction in (15)); the agreement on the auxiliary is controlled by the A-argument (unlike in the canonical transitive construction in (15)); and both arguments are in the nominative. Causativization is periphrastic by means of ‘do’ (e.g. 72), which is also commonly used in complex predicates (e.g. 70). There are several labile verbs, e.g. ‘break’ in (44).

### Case system

Archi has the signature Nakh-Daghestanian nominal inflection design. It includes two largely independent paradigms - cases (alias syntactic cases, grammatical cases) and spatial forms (alias spatial cases, locative cases). Archi has ten (grammatical) cases: Nominative (unmarked), Ergative, Genitive, Dative, Comitative, Comparative, Causal 'because of X', Substitutive 'instead of X, on X's behalf', and Equative ‘like X’. Spatial forms are morphologically complex, involving one marker for spatial relation (traditionally called "localization") and one marker for directionality (traditionally called "orientation").

There are six markers for spatial relations: i) location on the upper surface of a landmark (‘on the table’, SUPER), ii) inside a hollow landmark (‘in the box’, IN), iii) between multiple landmarks ('between trees') or inside a mass ('in the water', INTER), iv) at a human landmark (as in ‘at my parents’ place’, HUMLOC), v) location on a vertical surface with a fixation on it (‘on the wall’, CONT), vi) location under a landmark (‘under the table’, SUB).

Only CONT, SUPER, IN and INTER are present in the dataset, with IN and INTER only present in purely spatial contexts (12, 14, 21, 104).
There are six values of directionality: essive (Place; unmarked), lative (Goal, LAT), elative (Source, EL), translative (Path), directive (Direction) and terminative (Limit). Only elative, lative and essive are attested in the dataset. Archi also has a large number of non-bound functional elements that show mixed properties of adverb and adpositions; of all these items, only two show up in the dataset as means of flagging (i.e. postnominally).

There are 16 different flags attested in the data.

## Verb lemmas

Citation forms provided in the dataset are infinitives. Archi has a large number of stative predicates, including most property words. Stative predicates lack infinitives; in such cases, the citation form provided equals the root. For the purposes of citation, complex predicates are used as citation form as such. 
The root of a stative predicate also happens to be the simplest finite form (present durative); cf. (6, 7); though they may also form imperfective converbs and combine with the auxiliary (5).The auxiliary neutrally follows the converb, and Gender 4 / NPL forms of the auxiliary i coalesces with the converb (e.g. 5, 9).
